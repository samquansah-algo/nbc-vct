# The Human Capability Utility

### A thesis for learning, distributed intelligence, and community-owned infrastructure in an age of abundant AI

> **Version 1 (September 2026).** Now the **main frame** of the [unified v3 thesis](../v3-unified/THESIS.md). Kept as a record; see [`../LINEAGE.md`](../LINEAGE.md). Text reproduced as written, with markdown formatting only.

---

## Thesis

The defining educational challenge of the coming decades may not be a shortage of intelligence.

Artificial intelligence is rapidly reducing the cost of generating explanations, software, simulations, media, feedback, analysis, and increasingly sophisticated reasoning. If this trajectory continues, high-quality machine intelligence may become one of the most abundant cognitive resources humanity has ever produced.

But intelligence will not become abundant everywhere in the same way.

Compute, electricity, chips, connectivity, devices, capital, physical infrastructure, trusted institutions, and opportunities for meaningful human development will remain unevenly distributed. Hundreds of millions of children may therefore grow up during the greatest expansion of machine intelligence in history while remaining separated from the infrastructure through which that intelligence is accessed and transformed into human capability.

This creates a fundamental infrastructure problem.

The dominant response has been to extend the existing computing paradigm: connect every school, place a screen in front of every child, move learning resources to the cloud, and increasingly place an AI tutor behind the screen.

That may be valuable, but it assumes that the personal computer, smartphone, continuous internet connection, cloud application, and individual user account are the natural endpoints of educational computing.

They may not be.

The alternative thesis is that the next billion children may not each need to own increasingly powerful computers for computation to become ubiquitous in their lives.

Computation itself can escape the computer.

It can become distributed across inexpensive objects, materials, surfaces, rooms, tools, toys, public infrastructure, and environments. Expensive intelligence can be concentrated in shared local nodes. Physical experiences can become programmable. Human action can become machine-readable without requiring continuous audiovisual surveillance. Learning experiences can become executable rather than merely consumable. And the resulting infrastructure can become economically productive rather than existing only as a cost borne by schools and governments.

The resulting system would not simply be another educational technology.

It would constitute a new form of public and community infrastructure:

> **a Human Capability Utility.**

Its purpose would be to make intelligence accessible, make the physical world programmable for learning, generate trustworthy evidence of human capability, and allow schools and communities to participate economically in the emerging intelligence economy.

---

## I. The historical transition: from scarce intelligence to scarce capability

For most of human history, expertise was scarce.

Knowledge was expensive to produce, store, reproduce, distribute, and access. Schools therefore evolved partly as institutions for concentrating scarce knowledge and knowledgeable adults and distributing that knowledge to children.

The internet radically reduced the cost of distributing information.

Artificial intelligence goes further.

It reduces the cost of producing useful cognitive output.

A machine can increasingly explain a concept in thousands of ways, generate practice problems indefinitely, translate between languages, create software, produce images and simulations, analyze information, provide feedback, and adapt explanations to individuals.

This changes the fundamental scarcity around which education has been organized.

When explanations are scarce, delivering explanations creates value.
When content is scarce, producing content creates value.
When expertise is scarce, providing access to expertise creates value.

But when machine-generated intelligence becomes abundant, the scarce resource moves elsewhere.

What becomes scarce is the human ability to: form judgments, act under uncertainty, manipulate reality, collaborate with others, build things, discover problems, test hypotheses, persist through failure, transfer understanding between unfamiliar situations, exercise agency, and demonstrate that these capabilities genuinely belong to the human rather than the machine assisting them.

The frontier therefore moves from:

> access to information

to:

> access to intelligence

and eventually toward:

> development and verification of human capability.

This produces a paradox.

The more capable AI becomes at producing intellectual artifacts, the less those artifacts necessarily tell us about the human who presents them.

An essay is weaker evidence of writing capability when a machine can generate an essay.
Software is weaker evidence of programming capability when a machine can generate software.
A polished presentation is weaker evidence of synthesis when AI can produce the presentation.
Even correct answers become increasingly ambiguous evidence of understanding.

Artificial intelligence therefore creates both an intelligence abundance and an evidence crisis.

Future learning infrastructure must solve both.

---

## II. The central proposition

The central proposition is:

> **If machine intelligence becomes abundant while compute access, physical experience, trustworthy evidence of human capability, and opportunities for consequential action remain scarce, the foundational learning infrastructure of the next era should not merely distribute AI to children. It should convert abundant machine intelligence into distributed human capability.**

This requires four foundational technologies and one economic architecture:

- **Computational Matter** makes the physical environment programmable.
- **Computational Tokens** provide extremely inexpensive physical primitives through which people can manipulate computational systems.
- **The Capability Graph** provides a machine-readable representation of underlying human capabilities and their relationships.
- **Village AI** provides shared local intelligence, storage, orchestration, and inference without requiring one expensive computing system per person.
- **The Contribution and Settlement Layer** allows infrastructure owners, communities, educators, creators, and appropriately protected child-benefit structures to participate in the economic value generated by the network.

Together, they create the Human Capability Utility.

---

## III. Computational Matter: computation leaves the screen

The first architectural shift is from personal computing to environmental computing.

Today's dominant digital architecture concentrates interaction inside rectangular screens. The screen represents the world computationally.

A different architecture would make parts of the world itself computational.

Objects could possess identity.
Materials could retain state.
Surfaces could recognize arrangements.
Physical actions could trigger computational consequences.
Rooms could execute different rule systems.
Ordinary objects could acquire different meanings depending on the computational environment in which they are operating.

A block would therefore not inherently be a mathematics block, programming block, language block, or science block.

It would be a programmable physical primitive.

In one experience, it might represent water.
In another, an instruction.
In another, money.
In another, a molecule.
In another, a person.
In another, an argument.
In another, a machine component.

Meaning would reside primarily in the executed environment, not the manufactured object.

This is the core principle of computational matter:

> **Separate the physical interface from the experience it executes.**

That separation is important because it mirrors one of the most powerful abstractions in computing.

A computer does not need separate hardware for spreadsheets, games, communication, simulation, programming, and publishing.

Software changes what common hardware becomes.

Computational matter would attempt to bring the same property to physical learning environments.

---

## IV. The computational token: the smallest physical primitive

The second layer is the computational token.

The long-term objective would be an extraordinarily inexpensive, durable, standardized physical object containing only the minimum computation required to participate in a larger intelligent environment.

Depending on technical requirements, a token might eventually contain some combination of: identity, small amounts of memory, proximity or touch detection, basic communication, state indication, energy harvesting or extremely low-power operation.

The token itself does not need frontier intelligence.

That is precisely the point.

Today's architecture frequently concentrates:

> interface + storage + computation + connectivity + intelligence

inside one relatively expensive personal device.

The proposed architecture decomposes them.

The token provides the physical interface.
The local environment handles coordination.
Village AI supplies intelligence.
The Capability Graph supplies semantics.
The global network supplies optional frontier-scale computation.

This creates a different economic equation:

> **Do not replicate expensive intelligence inside every endpoint. Make endpoints radically inexpensive and share intelligence across them.**

A child might interact with fifty computational objects without owning fifty computers.

Twenty children might collectively manipulate hundreds of computational primitives connected to one local intelligence node.

Educational computing therefore changes from:

> one learner → one computer → one cloud account

toward:

> many humans → computational environment → shared intelligence.

---

## V. The Experience Protocol: separating infrastructure from learning

Hardware alone would create another educational kit.

The more important invention would be an open or standardized Experience Protocol.

The protocol would define how a computational environment describes: the world being instantiated; the objects available within it; their possible states; rules governing their interaction; challenges presented to participants; permissible actions; consequences of those actions; evidence generated by state changes; and capabilities that the experience is intended to investigate.

An experience could therefore become an executable package.

One might instantiate a village experiencing drought.
Another might instantiate an electrical system.
Another a marketplace.
Another a biological ecosystem.
Another a fictional civilization.
Another a machine.
Another a negotiation.
Another a programming environment.

The physical infrastructure remains largely unchanged.

What changes is the world being executed.

This distinction could create an ecosystem analogous to software.

Teachers could create experiences.
Children could create experiences.
Researchers could create controlled experimental environments.
Governments could publish public curricula as executable worlds.
Companies could build commercial experiences.
Communities could translate, modify, fork, and redistribute them.

Instead of millions of schools purchasing thousands of specialized educational objects, a common physical infrastructure could execute an expanding universe of experiences.

---

## VI. The Capability Graph: separate capability from experience

The third fundamental separation is between what a learner can do and the environment through which it was learned.

Traditional educational systems frequently organize knowledge according to institutional structures: grade levels, subjects, courses, chapters, units, lessons, examinations.

Those structures are useful organizational devices, but they do not necessarily constitute a computational representation of human capability.

The Capability Graph would attempt to represent relationships among capabilities themselves.

These could include increasingly sophisticated combinations of: observation, comparison, estimation, decomposition, sequencing, abstraction, causal reasoning, planning, coordination, explanation, model construction, debugging, optimization, judgment under uncertainty, creation, adaptation, and transfer.

The critical property is that multiple experiences can point toward overlapping capabilities.

A resource-allocation game involving water may exercise some of the same underlying reasoning as a spacecraft oxygen challenge.

A physical programming system might exercise capabilities also required for planning a complex sequence of real-world actions.

The system could therefore distinguish: **experience from capability.**

This enables one of the architecture's most important functions: **transfer testing.**

If a learner appears to demonstrate a capability in one environment, the system can deliberately generate a different environment in which superficial features change while the hypothesized underlying capability remains relevant.

Learning therefore becomes not simply:

> Can you repeat what you practiced?

but:

> **Does something persist when the world changes?**

Transfer can become an architectural property rather than an occasional research measurement.

---

## VII. Proof of Human Capability

The combination of programmable environments and the Capability Graph produces another potential infrastructure layer: **Proof of Human Capability.**

Physical environments generate sequences of consequential state changes.

A learner tries something.
The environment changes.
The learner responds.
A strategy fails.
The learner revises it.
Resources change.
The learner adapts.
A collaborator behaves unexpectedly.
The group reorganizes.
A familiar representation disappears.
The learner transfers or fails to transfer.

The resulting evidence is fundamentally different from a conventional answer sheet.

It represents a trajectory through a problem space.

Importantly, such evidence need not require continuous cameras, microphones, biometric monitoring, or exhaustive behavioral surveillance.

The architecture could intentionally capture the minimum necessary state transitions:

> state → human action → changed state → subsequent action.

The system observes primarily what happened to the computational environment, rather than attempting to record everything about the child.

From repeated experiences, the system could construct evidence-backed capability claims with explicit uncertainty.

Not:

> Mathematics: 82%.

But potentially:

> Demonstrated proportional reasoning across four substantially different environments; successful transfer observed in two unfamiliar contexts; evidence remains weak under time constraints.

Such claims would still require substantial scientific validation.

But if reliable, they could become increasingly important in an AI-rich world where finished digital artifacts alone provide weaker evidence of independent human capability.

## VIII. Village AI: intelligence becomes local infrastructure

The fourth component addresses the economics of AI.

Instead of assuming that every child continuously accesses frontier models through a personal device and broadband connection, intelligence can be architecturally distributed.

A Village AI, or Local Intelligence Node, could serve a school, neighborhood, library, refugee settlement, community center, or cluster of institutions.

The node could provide: local inference, model storage, experience execution, capability-graph services, teacher tools, knowledge retrieval, translation, evidence processing, synchronization, and community computing.

Connectivity would improve the system without necessarily being required for every interaction.

Large models, software, learning experiences, and updates could periodically synchronize.

Routine inference could occur locally.

Extremely demanding tasks could reach larger regional or global infrastructure when connectivity permits.

The resulting topology becomes:

> cheap ubiquitous interfaces → shared local intelligence → intermittent regional/global intelligence → frontier compute.

This is fundamentally different from making every physical interaction a cloud request.

It also makes AI infrastructure a shared capital asset.

---

## IX. The school becomes an intelligence node

This leads to the economic transformation at the center of the thesis.

Schools are conventionally treated economically as consumers of infrastructure.

Governments, parents, donors, and philanthropies provide resources.

Schools consume electricity, devices, connectivity, software, instructional materials, and labor to produce education.

But a school equipped with energy, connectivity, compute, storage, physical interfaces, community knowledge, human expertise, and an intelligence node possesses potentially productive infrastructure.

During school hours it supports learning.

Outside school hours it could, under appropriate governance, support: community AI access, local businesses, adult learning, entrepreneurship, agricultural or health information services, local knowledge systems, teacher development, computational services, and other locally valuable applications.

The infrastructure could therefore have utilization far beyond the school day.

This changes the financing question.

Instead of asking:

> Can a low-resource school afford AI infrastructure?

ask:

> **Can intelligence infrastructure produce sufficient educational, social, computational, and economic value over its useful life to justify financing it as a productive community asset?**

That moves the problem from educational procurement toward infrastructure economics.

---

## X. From consumers of AI to participants in the intelligence economy

The architecture also reverses the direction of value creation.

Today's dominant AI infrastructure is highly centralized.

Capital finances data centers.
Data centers produce compute.
AI laboratories create models.
Models reach users through applications.
Schools and communities sit near the consumption end of this chain.

A distributed Human Capability Utility creates another possibility.

Communities could contribute valuable intellectual assets upward while receiving intelligence downward.

But the economic proposition should not depend on commodifying children's private behavior.

The valuable contributions can instead include: locally created learning worlds; human-designed evaluation environments; translations; locally governed knowledge; model evaluations; community-developed benchmarks; verified public-interest datasets; teacher-created materials; physical-world problem environments; and novel solutions created through the network.

This matters particularly as AI advances.

Frontier systems will require increasingly difficult ways of determining whether models actually generalize beyond familiar digital benchmarks.

A geographically distributed network of programmable physical environments could potentially provide something different: **a distributed infrastructure for evaluating intelligence against diverse, unfamiliar, consequential environments grounded in the physical and social world.**

The school therefore need not sell children's data.

It can provide infrastructure, environments, knowledge, creativity, evaluation capacity, and other governed contributions.

---

## XI. The Contribution Protocol

For this to create economic participation, the network requires a Contribution Protocol.

The protocol would distinguish between participation and economically valuable contribution.

Examples might include:

- A teacher creates an experience subsequently adopted by 50,000 schools.
- A community develops and validates a language resource.
- A school creates a physical-world AI evaluation environment.
- A group develops a new solution to an engineering challenge.
- A university validates a capability assessment.
- A local provider provides compute to neighboring institutions.
- A developer improves the Experience Protocol.
- A community contributes a carefully governed dataset to a research commons.

Each contribution can have: provenance, consent, ownership, permitted uses, quality measures, attribution, licensing conditions, and compensation rules.

This is critical.

The economic primitive should not be:

> human activity = monetizable data.

It should be:

> **governed contribution = attributable value.**

---

## XII. The economic token is not the child

The word "token" therefore requires careful separation.

There may eventually be at least three different primitives.

1. **Computational Token:** the inexpensive physical interface.
2. **Intelligence Credit:** a unit representing access to compute or inference.
3. **Contribution Credit:** a unit representing an attributable contribution to the network.

None requires turning a child into a speculative financial asset.

Children's participation requires substantially stronger safeguards than ordinary commercial participation.

Where economic value arises from environments involving minors, benefits could flow through structures such as schools, community trusts, scholarships, project funds, learning credits, child-benefit accounts, or other legally and ethically appropriate mechanisms.

The objective is not: financialize childhood.

It is: **ensure that communities creating value within the intelligence economy participate in that value rather than merely supplying uncompensated inputs to centralized platforms.**

---

## XIII. Community intelligence as an investable infrastructure asset

This opens an entirely different capital model.

Suppose a Local Intelligence Node requires capital expenditure for: compute, energy, networking, computational matter, installation, maintenance, and software.

Instead of funding this exclusively through annual education budgets, infrastructure could potentially be financed through blended structures involving: governments, development finance institutions, philanthropy, AI companies, telecommunications providers, impact investors, diaspora capital, local cooperatives, and communities.

Returns would need to be separated into different categories.

Some are financial.
Some are educational.
Some are public goods.
Some are avoided costs.
Some are infrastructure assets remaining under community ownership.

A credible model would therefore measure total infrastructure productivity, not simply software revenue per student.

The relevant question becomes:

> **How much human, computational, social, and economic value can one dollar of local intelligence infrastructure generate over its lifetime?**

That may produce very different investment decisions from asking how much a school can pay for another educational subscription.

---

## XIV. The intelligence grid

At scale, these nodes could form a hierarchical distributed network.

- **Global layer:** frontier models, enormous data centers, foundational research, global knowledge, and high-compute workloads.
- **Regional layer:** model distribution, aggregation, specialized compute, governance, language infrastructure, and network coordination.
- **Local layer:** Village AI, storage, local models, community knowledge, privacy controls, capability services, and economic settlement.
- **Environmental layer:** computational matter, tokens, surfaces, tools, rooms, machines, and other programmable environments.
- **Human layer:** children, teachers, families, creators, workers, researchers, entrepreneurs, and communities.

Intelligence travels downward.

Governed knowledge, evaluation, innovation, and contributions can travel upward.

Economic value can move in both directions.

This begins to resemble a grid.

Electricity infrastructure separated:

> generation → transmission → distribution → meter → appliance.

The Human Capability Utility might separate:

> frontier intelligence → distributed compute → local intelligence → experience protocol → computational matter → human action.

And in reverse:

> human action → evidence → capability → contribution → network knowledge → economic value.

---

## XV. A different relationship between AI and childhood

The deepest implication is not technological.

It concerns what role AI should occupy in childhood.

The prevailing AI education model increasingly places intelligence directly between the child and the problem:

> child → AI → answer.

A Human Capability Utility can intentionally produce another relationship:

> **child → world → action → consequence → reflection → AI.**

AI does not necessarily eliminate struggle.

It orchestrates productive struggle.

It does not necessarily provide the solution.

It can alter the environment.
It can introduce uncertainty.
It can change constraints.
It can generate another world.
It can ask for an explanation.
It can determine what evidence is still missing.
It can help a teacher understand what happened.

And when appropriate, it can teach.

The objective becomes not maximizing AI assistance.

It becomes **maximizing the conversion of machine intelligence into independent human capability.**

---

## XVI. The network effect

If the architecture works, several network effects could reinforce one another.

More nodes create more environments.
More environments create more opportunities to investigate capability.
More creators produce more experiences.
More experiences increase the usefulness of common computational matter.
More implementations improve the Capability Graph.
More communities introduce greater linguistic, geographic, material, and problem diversity.
Greater diversity creates harder tests of both human transfer and machine generalization.
Better evaluation makes the network more useful to AI developers and researchers.
Economic value can finance additional infrastructure.
Additional infrastructure brings more communities into the network.

The flywheel becomes:

> **Infrastructure → Experiences → Human capability → Evidence → Knowledge and contributions → Network value → Investment → More infrastructure.**

If that loop closes economically, the system moves beyond philanthropy-dependent educational technology.

---

## XVII. The trillion-dollar question is not whether the token works

The $1 token is an implementation hypothesis.

The Village AI is an implementation hypothesis.

Even the Capability Graph is an implementation hypothesis.

The deeper thesis is independent of any one technology:

> **Can intelligence infrastructure be decomposed so that expensive intelligence is shared, interfaces become radically inexpensive, the physical world becomes programmable, and meaningful human action becomes trustworthy evidence of capability?**

And then:

> **Can the resulting infrastructure create sufficient value that communities become owners and productive participants in the intelligence economy rather than permanent customers at its edge?**

Those are the propositions worth falsifying.

---

## XVIII. The scientific questions

The thesis contains several claims that should not be assumed.

They should be experimentally attacked.

- **Learning:** Can capability developed through programmable physical environments transfer reliably to substantially different environments?
- **Measurement:** Can state transitions in physical systems provide valid evidence about underlying capability?
- **AI:** Can local models orchestrate useful adaptive environments with dramatically less compute than continuous frontier-model access?
- **Hardware:** Can computational matter become cheap, durable, repairable, interoperable, and manufacturable at meaningful scale?
- **Protocol:** Can sufficiently different learning experiences share a common execution architecture?
- **Capability representation:** Can capabilities be represented usefully without reducing complex human development to simplistic machine-readable metrics?
- **Economics:** Can shared intelligence infrastructure produce enough utilization and value to lower total cost per beneficiary?
- **Governance:** Can communities meaningfully control contributions, data, models, and economic participation?
- **Child protection:** Can useful evidence be produced without constructing pervasive surveillance systems around children?
- **AI frontier value:** Will geographically and materially diverse physical-world evaluations remain sufficiently valuable to advanced AI systems to support an external market?

The architecture becomes scientifically interesting precisely because these claims can fail.

## XIX. The first falsifiable system

The first prototype should therefore not attempt to build the global network.

Build one microcosm.

- One Local Intelligence Node.
- Approximately 20–50 computational tokens.
- One programmable physical surface.
- One minimal Experience Protocol.
- One provisional Capability Graph containing perhaps five deeply specified capabilities.
- Three radically different physical worlds.
- One locally running AI model.
- A small cohort of learners.

Teach or develop a capability primarily through World A.

Measure performance in an unfamiliar World B.

Then substantially change representation and context again in World C.

Capture only the minimum necessary physical state transitions.

Test whether those trajectories predict successful transfer better than simpler measures.

Then determine whether the AI can use that evidence to select or generate a subsequent experience that reveals something genuinely new about the learner's capability.

The first milestone is therefore not:

> Children enjoyed the product.

Nor:

> The AI generated good lessons.

Nor:

> The hardware worked.

The critical result is:

> **We can execute different physical worlds on common infrastructure, observe consequential human action, and obtain meaningful evidence about whether an underlying capability transfers between those worlds.**

If that does not hold, the larger thesis requires substantial revision.

If it does, the economic and infrastructural possibilities become worth investigating.

---

## XX. The long-term proposition

Imagine millions of compatible nodes.

A school in Cape Coast operates the same underlying protocol as a library in Nairobi, a learning center in Dhaka, a community space in São Paulo, and a school in Boston.

Their physical materials need not look identical.
Their languages need not be identical.
Their experiences need not be identical.
Their curricula need not be identical.
Their AI models need not even be identical.

What they share are protocols allowing environments to execute experiences, objects to communicate state, evidence to be represented, capabilities to be investigated, contributions to retain provenance, and intelligence to move through the network.

Children can create worlds rather than merely consume them.
Teachers can publish and fork experiences.
Researchers can run carefully governed experiments.
Communities can maintain local knowledge.
AI systems can encounter environments far outside their original training distributions.
Schools can provide community compute.
Creators can participate economically when their contributions generate value.

And learners can accumulate evidence of capability that is not reducible to the digital artifacts AI can increasingly produce on their behalf.

At that point, the infrastructure is no longer properly described as EdTech.

It becomes part of the intelligence infrastructure of society.

---

## Conclusion: from data centers to human capability

The AI era is producing extraordinary investment in the infrastructure required to create machine intelligence: data centers, energy generation, semiconductor fabrication, networks, models, and compute.

But societies will require an equally serious answer to another question:

> **What infrastructure converts abundant machine intelligence into abundant human capability?**

The answer may not be another screen.

It may not be another chatbot.

It may not even resemble today's idea of a computer.

The alternative proposed here is a distributed Human Capability Utility in which computation permeates inexpensive physical matter; shared local AI makes advanced intelligence accessible without requiring expensive individual endpoints; a Capability Graph separates what humans can do from the particular environments in which they learned it; programmable experiences turn physical environments into executable worlds; privacy-preserving evidence makes transfer observable; and contribution protocols allow schools and communities to participate in the economic value of the intelligence network.

Its foundational economic proposition is equally important:

> **Educational infrastructure should not have to remain a terminal consumption expense at the edge of the AI economy. It can become productive, locally owned intelligence infrastructure.**

And its foundational developmental proposition is:

> **The purpose of abundant artificial intelligence should not be to remove human action from learning, but to create environments in which human agency, judgment, creation, adaptation, and transfer can become more abundant.**

The ultimate architecture is therefore not: one child, one computer.

Nor: one child, one AI tutor.

It is:

> **abundant intelligence, distributed computation, programmable reality, verifiable human capability, and community participation in the value created.**

- The $1 computational token is merely the smallest visible object in that system.
- The Village AI is its local intelligence.
- The Capability Graph is its semantic substrate.
- The Experience Protocol is its execution layer.
- The Contribution Protocol is its economic coordination layer.
- The community is its infrastructure owner.
- And the human being, rather than the machine, remains the ultimate unit of development.

The bet is not that we can build a cheaper computer for the next billion children.

The bet is that we can build the infrastructure through which the next billion children become capable participants, creators, and owners in an age of abundant intelligence.
